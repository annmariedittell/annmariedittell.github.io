Accessibility Strategy Case Study

Using Accessibility Data to Drive Organizational Change

Turning recurring accessibility issues into cross-project standards, reusable guidance, and more consistent delivery across teams.

My Role
Senior Accessibility Engineer
Scope
Six large digital projects plus company-wide internal support
Focus
Accessibility strategy, defect analytics, systemic improvement
Outcome
Cross-project standards, reusable guidance, and a company-wide internal Accessibility Hub

Challenge

Accessibility issues were being identified across multiple digital systems, but reviewing and remediating individual defects only addressed the immediate problem.

As patterns emerged, it became clear that some issues were not isolated. They reflected recurring design, development, and implementation practices that could continue to create accessibility barriers across projects.

Fixing each defect individually would improve the affected screen or application, but it would not address the underlying cause. The larger opportunity was to understand why the same types of issues kept appearing, where teams were struggling, and what changes could improve accessibility across multiple projects at once.

My Role

As Senior Accessibility Engineer, I led a cross-project effort to understand why accessibility issues were recurring and where teams needed greater support.

I met with teams across six large digital projects to understand their accessibility challenges, listen to their pain points, and learn where existing processes, guidance, or tools were falling short. I combined those conversations with accessibility defect data and testing trends to identify patterns that extended beyond any one application or team.

That broader view helped me distinguish isolated defects from systemic problems. I looked for common root causes, gaps in team knowledge, recurring implementation patterns, and opportunities to address accessibility earlier in design and development.

I then translated those findings into organizational improvements, including reusable accessibility standards, practical guidance, and shared resources that could support multiple projects instead of solving the same problems one team at a time.

This work ultimately contributed to the development of a company-wide internal Accessibility Hub, giving employees and project teams a central place to find accessibility guidance and helping move the organization toward a more consistent, proactive approach to accessibility.

Finding the System Behind the Defects

Rather than treating every accessibility finding as an isolated ticket, I looked for patterns across projects and teams.

  • Are we seeing the same accessibility issue repeatedly?
  • Is the issue connected to a shared component, design pattern, development practice, or misunderstanding of accessibility requirements?
  • Are multiple teams struggling with the same problem?
  • Would shared guidance or a reusable standard prevent the issue from recurring?
  • Could the issue be addressed earlier in design or development?

This shifted the conversation from “How do we fix this defect?” to “Why does this keep happening, and what can we change so it does not continue across projects?”

One example was the identification of four major accessibility defects in a shared design system. I worked with teams to remediate the shared components, improving accessibility across our six projects and extending the benefit to other vendor teams using the same design system.

Turning Insights into Shared Standards

Once recurring patterns and common pain points were identified, I translated the findings into practical resources teams could use in their day-to-day work.

The goal was not simply to point teams back to WCAG or Section 508 requirements. The guidance needed to make those requirements understandable and actionable within the context of the products teams were actually designing, developing, and testing.

The resulting resources included accessibility standards, implementation guidance, testing information, and practical recommendations that could be reused across projects.

By creating shared guidance instead of relying on one-off answers, accessibility knowledge became easier to find, easier to apply, and less dependent on an accessibility specialist being present for every decision.

Building an Organization-Wide Accessibility Hub

The need for a more consistent source of accessibility information led to the development of a company-wide internal Accessibility Hub.

The Hub brought accessibility guidance and resources together in one place so employees and project teams across the company could more easily find information related to design, development, testing, and accessibility requirements.

This created a shared foundation that could support multiple projects and helped reduce the need for teams to repeatedly solve the same accessibility questions independently.

More importantly, it helped shift accessibility from something addressed primarily through defect remediation to something teams could reference throughout the product lifecycle.

Moving Accessibility Earlier

The defect data and team feedback also helped identify opportunities to address accessibility earlier.

Recurring issues could be discussed during design reviews, component evaluations, development conversations, and quality processes instead of waiting until accessibility testing near the end of delivery.

This supported a more shift-left approach where accessibility findings from one project could inform future work across many projects.

The goal was not only to resolve existing defects, but to reduce the likelihood that the same barriers would be introduced again.

Outcome

This work helped move accessibility from a series of individual remediation activities toward a more consistent organizational practice, while shared-component fixes created impact beyond a single project or team.

Teams had shared guidance they could reference, recurring accessibility problems became easier to recognize, and accessibility findings could be used to improve future design and development decisions rather than simply documenting problems after they occurred.

The effort created a stronger feedback loop across projects:

  1. Listen to teams
  2. Analyze defect data
  3. Identify patterns
  4. Understand root causes
  5. Create shared guidance
  6. Improve future delivery
Key takeaway: Accessibility data is most valuable when it leads to organizational change. By combining defect trends with direct input from teams, I was able to identify systemic barriers, create reusable solutions, and help move accessibility from reactive remediation toward a more proactive and sustainable practice.
